Yes — **that is exactly how a real network engineer should start**.

You should **not start by randomly placing routers and switches and configuring technologies**. First, imagine you received requirements from a customer. Then you translate those requirements into:

**Business Requirements → Technical Requirements → Network Design → Implementation Plan → Configuration → Testing**

For your topology, here is a realistic customer scenario that would justify **why you designed the lab this way**.

# Customer Network Requirements for This Lab

## 1. Customer Background

The customer is a medium-sized company called **ABC Corporation**.

They have:

* One **Headquarters (HQ)**
* One **Branch Office**
* An **Internet Service Provider/WAN provider**
* Multiple departments at HQ
* Servers and internal services
* A need for secure communication between HQ and Branch
* A requirement for redundancy in the campus network
* Different routing domains because the network has grown over time

The customer wants a new enterprise network designed so it can:

1. Support current users and devices
2. Separate departments
3. Provide redundant switching paths
4. Continue operating if one network link fails
5. Connect HQ and Branch securely
6. Connect to multiple external providers/networks
7. Support dynamic routing
8. Scale in the future

---

# Phase 0 — Customer Discovery and Requirements Gathering

Before touching a router or switch, you meet with the customer.

## Customer Requirement 1: Department Separation

The customer says:

> "We have multiple departments. We do not want all computers on one flat network."

### Business requirement

Separate users into different network segments.

### Technical requirement

Use:

* VLANs
* Separate IP subnets
* Layer 3 routing between VLANs
* ACLs later if security policies require communication restrictions

### Why this appears in your design

This is why your LAN starts with switches and VLANs.

For example:

| Department | VLAN | Example Subnet |
| ---------- | ---: | -------------- |
| Users      |   10 | 10.10.10.0/24  |
| Servers    |   20 | 10.10.20.0/24  |
| Voice      |   30 | 10.10.30.0/24  |
| Management |   99 | 10.10.99.0/24  |

So the first design decision is:

> **The network cannot be one large Layer 2 broadcast domain.**

Therefore, you build VLANs.

---

# Customer Requirement 2: Redundant Campus Network

The customer says:

> "If one switch or cable fails, we do not want the entire office to lose connectivity."

This is extremely important.

### Business requirement

The network must have redundancy.

### Technical requirement

You design:

* Two distribution switches:

  * `HQ-DSW1`
  * `HQ-DSW2`
* Multiple uplinks
* EtherChannels/Port-Channels
* Spanning Tree protection
* First-hop gateway redundancy if required

### Why your topology has multiple switches

Instead of this:

```text
PCs
 |
ASW
 |
DSW
 |
Router
```

You design something closer to:

```text
           HQ-DSW1 -------- HQ-DSW2
             ||                ||
             ||                ||
           HQ-ASW1 -------- HQ-ASW2
```

The exact physical design can vary, but the customer requirement is:

> **There must not be a single cable failure that disconnects an entire switch block unnecessarily.**

That requirement leads directly to your use of **Port-Channels**.

---

# Customer Requirement 3: Increase Bandwidth Between Switches

The customer says:

> "Our users access servers and applications heavily. A single uplink may become congested."

### Business requirement

Increase available bandwidth and provide link redundancy.

### Technical requirement

Use EtherChannel.

This justifies:

* `Po1`
* `Po11`
* `Po12`
* `Po21`
* `Po22`

For example:

```text
DSW1 ===== Port-Channel ===== DSW2
          Po1
```

Multiple physical interfaces operate as one logical interface.

### Why not just use five individual links?

Because independent Layer 2 links can create loops.

EtherChannel allows links to be logically bundled.

So your customer requirement becomes:

> **The switching infrastructure must provide additional uplink capacity and survive the loss of an individual physical link.**

# Customer Requirement 4: Devices Must Communicate Between VLANs

The customer says:

> "Users in one department need access to shared resources located in another network."

For example:

```text
VLAN 10 User
     |
     |
Needs access to
     |
     v
VLAN 20 Server
```

### Technical requirement

The network needs Layer 3 routing.

You therefore configure:

* SVIs on multilayer switches
* IP addresses for VLAN gateways
* Dynamic routing between Layer 3 devices

For example:

```text
interface vlan 10
 ip address 10.10.10.1 255.255.255.0

interface vlan 20
 ip address 10.10.20.1 255.255.255.0
```

This explains why **SVIs belong in Phase 1**.

# Customer Requirement 5: The Campus Must Use Dynamic Routing

The customer says:

> "We expect the network to grow. We do not want engineers manually entering every route on every router."

### Business requirement

The internal network must scale.

### Technical requirement

Use a dynamic routing protocol.

For your design, you selected:

**EIGRP AS 90**

between the campus Layer 3 switches and the internal routing infrastructure.

### Why EIGRP here?

In your lab scenario, the company already uses EIGRP internally.

Therefore, you are not choosing EIGRP randomly.

The customer might say:

> "Our existing campus routing domain uses EIGRP AS 90, and the new network must integrate with it."

That is a realistic reason.

Your design becomes:

```text
HQ VLANs
   |
   v
HQ-DSW1 ===== HQ-DSW2
      \     /
       \   /
      EIGRP AS 90
         |
         v
     Core-Router
```

# Customer Requirement 6: Separate HQ and Branch Routing Domains

The customer has two locations:

```text
        HQ
         |
    Enterprise WAN
         |
      Branch
```

The customer says:

> "HQ and the Branch are separate network locations. We want routing designed in a scalable hierarchical way."

### Technical requirement

Use OSPF with multiple areas.

For example:

* HQ router side → OSPF Area 10
* Branch router side → OSPF Area 20
* WAN/transit backbone → OSPF Area 0

This gives a design such as:

```text
Campus
  |
EIGRP 90
  |
Core Router
  |
Redistribution / Routing Boundary
  |
OSPF Area 10
  |
HQ Router
```

And:

```text
Branch Router
     |
OSPF Area 20
```

# Important: Why Use Both EIGRP and OSPF?

This is a very important **customer requirement/design justification**.

You should not say:

> "I used EIGRP and OSPF because I wanted to practice both."

That is fine for a lab, but it is **not a real-world design justification**.

Instead, your customer scenario should be:

> **ABC Corporation has an existing EIGRP AS 90 campus environment at HQ. The company acquired a branch network that uses OSPF. The new enterprise design must allow the two routing domains to communicate.**

Now you have a legitimate reason for:

```text
EIGRP AS 90
     |
     |
Routing Boundary
     |
     |
OSPF
```

This also gives you an opportunity to learn:

* Route redistribution
* Administrative distance
* Route filtering
* Route summarization
* Avoiding routing loops

Those are much more CCNP-level topics.

# Customer Requirement 8: Internet/WAN Must Not Carry Internal Routing Unprotected

The customer says:

> "Traffic between HQ and the Branch may cross an external provider network. We need logical separation and secure transport."

* GRE Tunnel

# Why Front-Door VRF?

The problem can be explained like this.

Your router needs to know two different things:

### Underlay

How do I reach the **tunnel destination through the provider**?

```text
HQ Router
    |
Provider/WAN
    |
Branch Router
```

### Overlay

How do internal networks communicate through the GRE tunnel?

```text
HQ Internal Networks
        |
     GRE Tunnel
        |
Branch Internal Networks
```

FVRF separates the routing used to reach the tunnel destination from routing inside the tunnel.

Conceptually:

```text
           UNDERLAY
       FVRF Routing Table
HQ ---------------------- Branch
 \                         /
  \____ Provider Network __/


           OVERLAY
     Global / Overlay Routing

HQ LAN === GRE Tunnel === Branch LAN
```

This is a more advanced enterprise requirement.

## The most important engineering concept

Your design story should always be:

> **Customer requirement → technical requirement → technology selection → design → implementation → validation**

For example:

> **Customer:** "We cannot lose connectivity if one uplink cable fails."
> **Technical requirement:** Redundant Layer 2 uplinks are required.
> **Technology:** EtherChannel and STP.
> **Design:** Dual distribution/access connectivity with Port-Channels.
> **Implementation:** Configure `Po11`, `Po12`, etc.
> **Validation:** Shut down one member link and confirm connectivity continues.

One correction I strongly recommend: make your project **4 phases instead of 3**, because troubleshooting **BGP + FVRF + GRE + OSPF all at once** will be unnecessarily difficult. Build and validate the underlay first, then build the overlay on top of it.
